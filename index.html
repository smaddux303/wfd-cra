Introduction

Who We Are

Westminster Fire Department is a career, all-hazards fire and emergency medical services agency serving the City of Westminster, Colorado. Established in 1934, WFD has grown from a volunteer department into a nationally accredited agency — achieving ISO Class 1 status in 2017 and CFAI accreditation through the Commission on Fire Accreditation International in 2019.

This Community Risk Assessment represents our commitment to transparency. It is an honest examination of who lives in Westminster, what risks they face, and how WFD is organized and resourced to protect them — presented in a format that is accessible to residents, elected officials, and accreditation reviewers alike.

WFD operates across six fire districts from six strategically located stations, providing fire suppression, advanced life support emergency medical services, technical rescue, hazardous materials response, dive and swift water rescue, wildland firefighting, and emergency management services.

How We Measure Community Risk

1

Probability — How likely is this type of incident to occur in Westminster? Based on five years of actual incident history.

2

Consequence — If this incident occurs, how severe could the impact be? We consider life safety, property loss, and environmental harm.

3

Community Impact — What is the potential effect on Westminster as a whole? Infrastructure, economy, and public health are all considered.

4

Risk Score — These three factors are combined using Heron's Formula to produce a final community risk score, rated High, Medium, or Low.

Community Characteristics

Westminster, Colorado at a Glance

Westminster covers 34 square miles in the northern Denver metro area, spanning portions of both Adams and Jefferson counties. Its location at the convergence of two major interstate corridors and proximity to Rocky Mountain Regional Airport shapes both the character of the community and the risks WFD responds to.

Natural Hazards

Westminster's Hazard Profile

Westminster's Front Range location exposes the community to a broad range of natural hazards. The City's 2023 Hazard Mitigation Plan assessed 15 natural hazards using likelihood of occurrence (A–E) and potential impact (1–5). Since 2018, wildfire risk has been elevated significantly following the 2021 Marshall Fire — a watershed moment for Front Range communities. Since 1965, Adams and Jefferson counties have received 25 presidential disaster declarations.

Drought & Water Security

Westminster's semi-arid climate makes drought inevitable — roughly once per decade. Colorado was in drought 50 of the past 126 years. A multi-decade drought would threaten Standley Lake and the entire Front Range water supply. Water demand may surpass supply as soon as 2025 in the South Platte Basin.

WFD impact: wildfire risk, dehydration EMS calls, water supply for firefighting
Likelihood A · Impact A5 — Very High Consequence

Dam Failure

Westminster owns Ketner and McKay dams. Standley Lake (High Hazard), McKay Lake South (High Hazard), and Woman Creek (High Hazard) all have Emergency Action Plans. A Standley Lake failure would destroy several hundred homes and endanger the city's primary water supply.

WFD impact: mass casualty event, swift water rescue, mutual aid activation
Likelihood C · Impact C5 — High Consequence (elevated since 2021)

Open Space Fire / Wildfire

Wildfire risk was elevated significantly in the 2023 HMP following the 2021 Marshall Fire, which destroyed over 1,000 homes in Boulder County. Westminster has open space interface in northern districts and deploys resources statewide and nationally. WFD maintains Type 6 brush trucks and participates in regional wildland response.

WFD impact: structure protection, regional deployment, multi-day operations
Likelihood D · Impact D4 — Significant

Flooding

Westminster sits at the headwaters of Big Dry Creek and Little Dry Creek. Intense rain events (2–3 inches in one hour) can produce 100-year flood conditions rapidly. Nine presidential flood declarations since 1965. Adams County could experience $30 million in future flood losses under moderate climate scenarios.

WFD impact: swift water rescue, vehicle extrication, infrastructure disruption
Likelihood D · Impact D4 — Significant

Extreme Cold & Severe Winter Storms

Seven recorded extreme cold events since 1998. Blizzards are Westminster's most common meteorological hazard. While winters are trending warmer and shorter, extreme events remain a significant threat — particularly to vulnerable populations including elderly residents and those experiencing homelessness.

WFD impact: hypothermia EMS calls, vehicle accidents, hazardous road conditions
Likelihood C · Impact C5 — Significant

Extreme Heat

Climate change is increasing the frequency and intensity of extreme heat events on the Front Range. Heat index above 103°F poses life-threatening risk, especially for elderly, outdoor workers, and those without air conditioning. February 2023 was the fourth-warmest on global record — 528 consecutive months above the 20th century average.

WFD impact: heat-related EMS calls surge, vulnerable population welfare checks
Likelihood C · Impact C5 — Significant

Epidemic / Pandemic

Based on five pandemics in the past 100 years, a pandemic occurs roughly every 20 years — a 5% probability in any given year. Westminster received four COVID-19 presidential declarations in 2020. Emerging and antibiotic-resistant diseases remain a perennial threat driven by global travel and climate-influenced vector habitats.

WFD impact: PPE demands, EMS surge, staffing shortages, mutual aid strain
Likelihood B · Impact B4 — Significant

Earthquake

The Golden Fault is the closest quaternary fault to Westminster. While large earthquakes are rare in Colorado, induced seismicity from oil and gas activity north and east of the city warrants monitoring. A 2,500-year probabilistic scenario projects significant building damage and economic losses for the region.

WFD impact: structure collapse, search & rescue, utility disruption, mass casualty
Likelihood B · Impact B4 — Significant

Severe Summer Storms, Hail & Lightning

Colorado's "hail alley" location produces some of the nation's highest hail frequencies. Lightning strikes and severe summer storms cause significant property damage and injury calls annually. These are rapid-onset events well understood by the public but persistently dangerous and economically disruptive.

WFD impact: structure fires, traumatic injuries, vehicle accidents, power outages
Likelihood B · Impact B4 — Significant

Tornado

Westminster has recorded tornado events dating to 1950 including one presidential declaration in 1965. While the probability of a major tornado is low, the consequences are severe — structure destruction, mass casualties, and infrastructure damage. The city's grid street pattern and open areas increase exposure.

WFD impact: mass casualty, heavy rescue, search & rescue, utility response

Cascading hazard note: Westminster's 2023 Hazard Mitigation Plan emphasizes that hazards are interconnected. Drought stresses vegetation and increases wildfire risk. Wildfire degrades watersheds and increases flooding. Flooding can threaten dams. Extreme heat exacerbates drought. Climate change is amplifying the frequency and severity of each of these hazards — making multi-hazard planning and all-hazards emergency response capability increasingly critical for WFD. This document should be read alongside Westminster's 2023 Hazard Mitigation Plan for full depth of analysis.

About these scores
Risk scores use Heron's Formula combining Probability (incident frequency), Consequence (severity by call type), and Impact (commitment time). Thresholds: HIGH ≥ 20 · MEDIUM 10–19.99 · LOW < 10. Scores are compared across all 6 districts — ⚑ outlier indicates this district is more than 1 standard deviation above the district mean for that category.

How Risk Is Calculated

Risk Score Methodology

District risk scores combine three factors — how often an incident type occurs, how severe it tends to be, and how much time it commits — into a single comparable score for each of nine incident categories across all six districts.

The Formula

Risk Score = √(P × C × I) × √(P + C + I) / 3

Each incident category in each district receives a Probability score (P), a Consequence score (C), and an Impact score (I) — each on a 0–10 scale. The three are combined using this geometric mean formula, which rewards categories that score high across all three dimensions rather than any single factor alone.

Probability

How often this incident type occurs in a district, based on actual incident counts since 2024. More frequent categories score higher.

Consequence

How severe this incident type tends to be — property loss, civilian deaths and injuries, and incident-specific severity factors. Scoring varies by category (see below).

Impact

How long units are committed on scene, on average, for this incident type. Longer commitments place greater demand on district resources.

Consequence Scoring by Category

Consequence is calculated differently for each incident category to reflect what actually drives severity for that type of call. Every score is normalized to a 0–10 scale.

🏠 Structure Fire

A structure fire is always high-consequence — multi-unit response, life-safety risk — regardless of outcome. Scoring starts with a base tier (5 for full structural involvement, 3 for confined/chimney fires) and adds property loss, fatality, and injury factors on top.

🔥 Outside/Vehicle Fire

Vehicle, dumpster, and outside fires are scored on property loss and casualties only — no structural involvement tier applies since these incidents don't involve occupied buildings.

🌲 Wildland Fire

Scored the same way as outside/vehicle fires — property loss and casualties — reflecting the open-land nature of these incidents.

🚑 Medical

Based on the highest-acuity procedure performed by EMS crews — from basic assessment up to CPR, defibrillation, and trauma activation — reflecting true patient severity rather than dispatch type alone.

⛑️ Rescue

Tiered by technical complexity — confined space, trench, and high-angle rescues score highest, down to standby and limited-access calls at the low end.

☣️ Hazardous Situation

Tiers reflect Westminster's actual operational experience: a typical gas leak is a single-engine, routine response and scores low, while a radiological or biological release requiring outside resources scores highest.

Risk scores are recalculated annually using all incident data collected since January 1, 2024. District scores are also compared statistically against the 6-district average — a district more than one standard deviation above the mean for a category is flagged as a statistical outlier in the District Profiles section above.

Glossary of Terms

Plain-language definitions of technical terms used throughout this site.

NFIRS

National Fire Incident Reporting System — the legacy national standard for recording fire department incident data, used by WFD through March 2026.

NERIS

National Emergency Response Information System — the new national incident reporting standard WFD transitioned to in March 2026, replacing NFIRS.

CFAI

Commission on Fire Accreditation International — the body that accredits fire departments based on community risk assessment and self-evaluation against industry standards, administered through the Center for Public Safety Excellence (CPSE).

Z-Score

A statistical measure of how far a district's score is from the 6-district average, expressed in standard deviations. A Z-score above +1.5 flags a district as a statistical outlier for that category.

Percentile Rank

A district's standing relative to the other five districts for a given category, shown as a number 1–6. A rank of 6 means that district has the highest score (highest risk) among all six; a rank of 1 means the lowest.

Heron's Formula

The geometric-mean equation used to combine Probability, Consequence, and Impact into a single risk score. Named for its mathematical resemblance to the classical formula for triangle area.

Normalized Consequence

A consequence score rescaled to a common 0–10 range so that categories with different raw scoring scales (for example, Structure Fire vs. Medical) can be compared and combined fairly.

Outlier

A district whose score for a given category is statistically unusual compared to the other five — specifically, more than one standard deviation above the 6-district mean. Outliers are flagged with a ⚑ symbol.

ACS

American Community Survey — an ongoing U.S. Census Bureau survey providing detailed demographic, social, and economic estimates, used throughout this site for population, income, insurance, and poverty data.

DRCOG

Denver Regional Council of Governments — the regional planning agency whose small-area population and household forecasts inform the projected growth figures shown in the District Profiles section.

ISO Rating

A fire protection class rating issued by the Insurance Services Office, used by insurers to help set property insurance rates. Ratings range from Class 1 (best) to Class 10. WFD holds an ISO Class 1 rating, a distinction held by only a small number of fire departments nationwide.
